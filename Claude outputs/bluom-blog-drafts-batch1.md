### Body

**What Are Macros? A Simple Guide to Protein, Carbs & Fat**

If you've spent any time around fitness or nutrition content, you've heard someone say "I'm hitting my macros today." It sounds technical, but the idea behind it is simple.

So, what are macros? Short for macronutrients, they're the three nutrients your body needs in large amounts to function: protein, carbohydrates, and fat. Unlike micronutrients (vitamins and minerals, needed in tiny amounts), macros are what actually supply your calories — and each one plays a distinct role in how your body looks, feels, and performs.

## The three macros, and what each one actually does

**Protein** builds and repairs tissue — muscle, skin, hair, enzymes, hormones. It's the macro most linked to feeling full after a meal, and the one most people training for strength or fat loss are told to prioritize. It provides about 4 calories per gram.

**Carbohydrates** are your body's preferred, fastest-access fuel source, especially for your brain and for anything moderate-to-high intensity — sprinting, heavy lifting, a fast-paced sport. Carbs get an undeserved reputation as "bad"; in reality, the type and amount matter far more than the label. Carbs also provide about 4 calories per gram.

**Fat** supports hormone production, helps you absorb certain vitamins (A, D, E, K), and is essential for long-term health — it isn't the enemy older diet culture made it out to be. Fat is the most calorie-dense macro at about 9 calories per gram, which is why "high fat" foods add up quickly.

## Why people track macros instead of just calories

Calories tell you *how much* you're eating. Macros tell you *what* you're eating. Two people can eat the same 2,000 calories and end up with very different results depending on whether those calories came mostly from protein and vegetables, or mostly from refined carbs and fat.

Tracking macros — even loosely — helps with a few common goals:

Building muscle usually means prioritizing protein intake (a common starting point is roughly 0.7–1g per pound of bodyweight, though individual needs vary). Losing fat usually means keeping protein high enough to preserve muscle while running a modest calorie deficit. General health goals often come down to hitting a reasonable protein target and not needing to think much further than that.

## How to find your macro ratio

There's no single "correct" split — a common general starting point some people use is roughly 30% protein, 40% carbohydrate, and 30% fat of total calories, then adjusted from there based on how your body responds, your training style, and your goals. Someone doing endurance training will typically want more carbohydrate than someone doing purely strength work, for example.

The easier starting point for most people is to set a protein target first (since it's the macro most linked to satiety and muscle retention), then split the remaining calories between carbs and fat based on preference and how you feel.

## The easiest way to actually track macros

Manually calculating grams of protein, carbs, and fat across every meal gets old fast — it's the single biggest reason people quit tracking within the first two weeks. Once you know what macros actually are, the goal isn't perfection — it's consistency. Most people find that tracking for a few weeks builds enough intuition that they can eyeball a plate and be reasonably close, even without logging every bite forever.

### Body

**How to Build Good Habits That Actually Stick**

Most people don't fail at building good habits because they lack willpower. They fail because they start too big, expect motivation to carry them past week two, and have no way of seeing progress when it's slow and invisible. Here's how to build good habits in a way that's actually built to survive a bad week.

## Start smaller than feels necessary

The single most common mistake is choosing a habit that's too ambitious for day one. "Work out for an hour every day" or "meditate for 20 minutes" sounds good on paper, but it requires a level of motivation you won't have every single day — and missing the target repeatedly is what kills the habit.

A more durable approach: shrink the habit until it feels almost too easy. Two push-ups. One glass of water before coffee. Two minutes of stretching. The goal in the first few weeks isn't the workout — it's proving to yourself that you show up, consistently, regardless of how you feel. You can scale the difficulty up once the habit itself is automatic.

## Attach the new habit to something you already do

Habits are far easier to build when they're linked to an existing routine rather than left to depend on remembering. This is often called "habit stacking" — after [existing habit], I will [new habit]. After I pour my morning coffee, I'll take my supplements. After I brush my teeth, I'll do a two-minute stretch. The existing habit becomes the trigger, so you're not relying on memory or motivation to start.

## Track the streak, not just the outcome

Outcome goals (lose 10 pounds, run a 5K) are slow to show progress and easy to lose motivation on. Process goals — did I show up today — give you a win every single day, which is what actually keeps people going during the weeks when the scale or the mirror isn't moving.

This is the core idea behind streak-based habit tracking: seeing an unbroken chain builds its own momentum, and — just as importantly — seeing a single missed day clearly (rather than letting it quietly slide into a missed week) makes it much easier to get back on track immediately instead of giving up entirely.

## Expect to miss days — plan for it

Research on habit formation (a widely cited 2009 study from University College London found habit automaticity took anywhere from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and behavior, averaging around 66) makes one thing clear: there's no fixed number of days after which a habit "locks in," and everyone's timeline is different. What matters more than perfection is what you do after a miss. One skipped day is a blip. Two in a row is the beginning of a new, worse habit. The single highest-leverage rule in habit building is simple: never miss twice.

## Make the habit visible

Habits you don't track are easy to lose track of — literally. If you can't see how many days in a row you've shown up, it's much harder to feel the momentum that keeps you going. This is exactly why Bluom's habit tracker is built around visible streaks rather than just a checklist: every habit you set up — supplements, water, steps, meditation, whatever you're building — shows its current streak front and center, so the small, boring, daily win is impossible to miss.

Good habits aren't built through motivation. They're built through a system that makes showing up easier than not showing up, and makes your progress impossible to ignore.

### Body

**How to Lower Your Resting Heart Rate Naturally**

Your resting heart rate (RHR) — how many times your heart beats per minute while you're completely at rest — is one of the simplest, most useful signals of cardiovascular fitness you have. A lower RHR generally means your heart is working more efficiently, pumping more blood per beat so it needs fewer beats per minute to do its job. Here's what actually moves that number, and what doesn't.

## What counts as a "normal" resting heart rate

For most adults, a normal resting heart rate falls somewhere between 60 and 100 beats per minute, according to the American Heart Association. Well-conditioned athletes often sit well below that range — sometimes 40 to 60 bpm — simply because a fitter heart is a stronger pump. If your RHR is consistently outside the normal range, or you notice a sudden, unexplained change, that's worth a conversation with a doctor rather than a lifestyle tweak.

## Cardiovascular exercise is the single biggest lever

Regular aerobic exercise — walking, running, cycling, swimming — is the most well-established way to lower resting heart rate over time. As your cardiovascular fitness improves, your heart's stroke volume (how much blood it pumps per beat) increases, so it doesn't need to beat as often at rest to circulate the same amount of blood. Most guidelines point to roughly 150 minutes of moderate-intensity cardio per week as a reasonable target, with measurable RHR changes typically showing up over weeks to months of consistency, not days.

## Sleep quality matters more than people expect

Poor or insufficient sleep keeps your nervous system in a slightly more activated state, which shows up directly as a higher resting heart rate the next morning. This is actually one of the most useful things about tracking RHR daily rather than just once: a night of bad sleep, elevated stress, illness, or overtraining often shows up as an elevated RHR before you consciously feel it — making it an early warning sign worth paying attention to, not just a fitness vanity metric.

## Stress and breathing techniques

Chronic stress keeps your sympathetic nervous system ("fight or flight") more active than it needs to be, which raises baseline heart rate. Slow, controlled breathing — inhaling for a count of 4, exhaling for a count of 6 or longer — activates the parasympathetic ("rest and digest") response and can produce a noticeable, fairly immediate drop in heart rate. Practiced consistently, it also appears to support lower RHR over time.

## Hydration and caffeine

Even mild dehydration makes your heart work harder to maintain blood volume and pressure, which nudges resting heart rate up. Excess caffeine, particularly later in the day, has a similar short-term effect for many people. Neither is a dramatic lever on its own, but both are easy, low-effort fixes.

## Track it daily to see what's actually working

The honest answer is that resting heart rate moves slowly and responds to a mix of factors — training, sleep, stress, hydration — which makes single-day readings hard to interpret on their own. What matters is the trend over weeks.

### Body

**Does Coffee Break a Fast? What Black Coffee Really Does**

It's one of the most common questions in intermittent fasting: does coffee break a fast, or is it safe to drink during your fasting window? The short answer is that black coffee, on its own, does not meaningfully break a fast for most people and most goals. Here's the actual reasoning, and where it gets more complicated.

## Why black coffee is generally fine

A fast is, at its core, about giving your body an extended period without incoming calories so it can shift into fat-burning and cellular repair processes like autophagy. Black coffee contains essentially zero calories — typically 2 calories or less per cup — which is well below the threshold most researchers and fasting protocols consider enough to trigger a meaningful insulin response or end a fasted state.

Coffee also contains caffeine and chlorogenic acid, both of which have been studied for potentially *supporting* some of the goals people fast for — caffeine is a mild appetite suppressant for many people, which can actually make a fasting window easier to get through, not harder.

## Where it gets more complicated

The moment you add anything to your coffee — cream, milk, sugar, flavored syrup, even a splash of plant milk — you're introducing calories, and depending on the amount, that can trigger an insulin response that technically breaks the fasted state. Even small amounts (a tablespoon of half-and-half, a packet of sugar) add up more than people expect.

Black coffee with a small amount of unsweetened almond milk (a few calories) is a much smaller disruption than a splash of regular creamer, but neither is truly "zero." If your goal is the strictest possible fast — for example, for a specific medical protocol or extended fast — plain black coffee or plain tea is the safer choice.

## Does coffee affect autophagy specifically?

Autophagy — your cells' internal "clean-up" process, one of the key longer-term benefits people fast for — is triggered by an extended lack of incoming nutrients, not caffeine specifically. Some early research actually suggests certain compounds in coffee may support autophagy-related pathways, though this research is still developing and shouldn't be the main reason to drink coffee while fasting. The main point stands: black coffee's negligible calorie count means it doesn't meaningfully interrupt the low-insulin, low-nutrient state that autophagy depends on.

## The practical takeaway

If you're doing a standard intermittent fasting protocol (16:8, 18:6, or similar) for general health, weight management, or energy, black coffee — or coffee with a very small amount of milk — is very unlikely to meaningfully affect your results. If you're doing a strict or extended fast for a specific medical or research-backed reason, stick to water, plain black coffee, or plain tea to be safe.
